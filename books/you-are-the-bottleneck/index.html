You Are the Bottleneck

by Kimani Upshur, M.Ed.

Personal GrowthCommercial Psychology

Marcus ran a fourteen-person remodeling company, and on one Tuesday morning three different jobs stood completely still — a carpenter waiting on a tile decision, a finished quote waiting on a signature, an invoice waiting on initials — and every stop had the same name on it: his. Nobody would have called it a bottleneck. They would have said Marcus was busy, or that Marcus cared about quality. Both were true. But underneath the truths was a shape: the company had exactly one door, and every decision, approval, and answer had to walk through it, one at a time, at the speed of one man's attention. You Are the Bottleneck is written for the person who is that door — at work, at home, in ministry — and it begins by making the queue visible: how work piles up behind a single person's attention, why a ninety-second approval can carry two days of waiting, and why working harder at the front of the line never shortens it.

Then it diagnoses, because "bottleneck" is four different problems wearing one name. The capacity bottleneck: more work that only you can do than there are hours in you. The knowledge bottleneck: every answer lives in your memory, so every question makes a pilgrimage. The permission bottleneck: nothing moves without your yes, in a queue where sampling shows you change fewer than one item in twenty. And the perfectionism bottleneck: nothing leaves until it passes your standard — the one that feels most like a virtue and trains the whole system into dependence. Each kind has its own tell, its own test — including what really happens when you are gone for three days — and its own cure, and applying the wrong cure wastes a season.

The second half is the dismantling, lever by lever. Decide at the lowest level, with your judgment published in advance as rules, thresholds, and defaults. Delegate outcomes instead of tasks, so the work leaves your head as well as your hands. Write down what only you know — the twenty questions that generate most of the pilgrimages — with the reasons and exceptions attached, where the questions arise. Redesign the flow so work visits you once, on purpose, or not at all. And hold the gains with a fifteen-minute weekly review, because queues regrow. The aim is not to care less or to vanish from your own system. It is to make "I don't know what we'd do without you" a sentence your system can finally answer — because you built the thing that answers it, and walked through the door into the larger work waiting on the other side.

Written from a Christian worldview — Scripture first.
